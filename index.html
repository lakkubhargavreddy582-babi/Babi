in older adults.

Recognising hearing loss early, reducing its impact, and connecting people to effective care.

A practical, person-centred overview for clinicians, caregivers & community health teams

Hearing connects us to people
01 · The clinical picture

Hearing loss is common—
but never trivial.

Age-related hearing loss can affect participation, safety, communication and overall wellbeing.

Why it matters

A major contributor to years lived with disability.

Hearing loss is often gradual and under-recognised. Its consequences can extend well beyond the ear.

1 in 3people aged 65+ have disabling hearing loss*
50%of adults over 75 have hearing difficulty*
2×higher risk of social isolation when unaddressed

*Global estimates vary by definition and population. Source: WHO, World Report on Hearing (2021).

The ageing ear

What changes with age?

  • 01Inner-ear hair cells decline. These sensory cells do not regenerate, reducing clarity—especially for high-frequency sounds.
  • 02Sound processing becomes less efficient. Following speech in background noise can become particularly tiring.
  • 03Other conditions can compound loss. Wax, infection, vascular disease and medicines may all contribute.
Patterns of hearing loss

Not every hearing problem is the same.

A

Conductive

Sound is blocked in the outer or middle ear. Common causes include wax, otitis media and otosclerosis.

B

Sensorineural

Damage to the cochlea or auditory nerve. Presbycusis is the most common pattern in later life.

C

Mixed

A combination of conductive and sensorineural components, requiring a tailored assessment.

Recognise the signs

Listen for changes in daily life.

Communication strain

Frequently asking for repetition, mishearing words, or turning up television volume.

Participation changes

Avoiding restaurants, groups, phone calls or familiar social activities.

Safety concerns

Missing doorbells, alarms, traffic cues or medication instructions.

“I hear people talking, but I cannot make out the words.”A common way patients describe reduced speech clarity.
02 · Assessment & action

Ask. Screen.
Refer when needed.

A short, respectful conversation can identify a problem that someone has normalised for years.

A simple first screen

Start with the patient’s experience.

“Do you have difficulty hearing conversations?”
“Do you struggle more when there is background noise?”
“Has anyone close to you mentioned your hearing?”
Practical prompt

“What situations make it hardest for you to hear or follow a conversation?”

Clinical assessment

A focused evaluation prevents missed, treatable causes.

History

Onset, laterality, tinnitus, dizziness, noise exposure, medicines and functional impact.

Otoscopy

Check for wax, infection, perforation, foreign body or other canal pathology.

Bedside tests

Whispered voice; tuning forks when appropriate; assess communication needs.

Audiology

Formal audiometry defines type and degree of loss and informs management.

Referral pathway

Know what needs urgent attention.

Sudden hearing loss Same-day urgent assessment
Unilateral / asymmetric loss ENT or audiology review
Tinnitus, vertigo, otorrhoea Evaluate associated symptoms
Gradual bilateral loss Routine audiology & rehabilitation
Treatment & support

Amplification is only one part of care.

The best plan combines clinical treatment with practical communication support and follow-up.

  • ✓Remove wax or treat reversible ear disease.
  • ✓Offer hearing aids and verify benefit after fitting.
  • ✓Consider assistive listening devices, captions and communication therapy.
Better conversations

Small changes make a large difference.

Face the personKeep your face visible; natural lip-reading cues support understanding.
Reduce background noiseMove away from TV, music and kitchen noise before important discussions.
Speak clearly, not loudlyUse a steady pace and short sentences—shouting can distort speech.
Confirm the planUse teach-back and provide important instructions in writing.
Prevention

Protect the hearing that remains.

Prevention is relevant at every age. Help patients make hearing protection an ordinary part of self-care.

01

Manage sound exposure

Use hearing protection around loud tools, concerts and machinery.

02

Review medicines

Consider possible ototoxicity when reviewing treatment choices.

03 · Person-centred care

Treat the hearing loss—
and the life around it.

Success is measured in confidence, connection and meaningful participation, not only decibels.

Key takeaways

Hearing well supports living well.

Normalise hearing conversations, check for reversible causes, and ensure every older adult can access timely, respectful support.
